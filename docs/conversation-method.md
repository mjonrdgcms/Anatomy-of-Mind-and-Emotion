# The Conversation Method: Taboos, Favourites, and the Missing Axis

What the app is for, in the author's words: help people identify which tools and weapons they have a taboo blocking. The taboo is usually visible as the predominant quality of someone they don't like. Taboos block the natural reframing. The app must be good at seeing where the source books apply, at asking questions that are not leading or self-fulfilling, and at asking questions that provoke insight. The core practice of the theory: identify your favourite three approaches, then find people in your life who are good at the ones you are not. When the app sees the user is not seeing one of the seven axes of value, it asks who they know who is good at that one, and what that person would likely do.

## 1. The model of a taboo

- A **taboo** is a whole approach the person will not use. It shows as a strong dislike of that approach's weapons: the person can name what they cannot stand in someone else, and it is always a weapon on the wheel.
- Because the shape is seven-sided, the two approaches **across the wheel** from the taboo are carrying the load. The person lives in their tools and, under pressure, in their weapons. This is the palette of *Beyond Flat Land*: two and a half approaches under load, the shadow appearing in caricature.
- A taboo blocks **reframing** because reframing needs the missing approach. You cannot let go of a boulder with the tools for carrying one. The heart keeps proposing the missing approach (the emotion, the dream), and the taboo keeps refusing it, so the message gets louder.
- The way out is not to practise the disliked weapon. It is to find a **person who uses the tool** on that side of the wheel well, and ask what they would do. The ch. 29 observation in *Beyond Flat Land*: watching somebody do well the thing you found contemptible has been the most effective intervention on the outside lock.

## 2. The three instruments

**The disliked-weapon survey.** Ask for the three weapons the person dislikes most, offered as the 56 weapon names (or as the 28 pair-weapons for a shorter version), not as approach names. Map each to its approach. The taboo is where the dislikes cluster. The load-bearing approaches are the two across from each. This was the author's early survey, and it was very predictive.

**The favourite three.** Ask the person to pick their three favourite approaches, described by what they do (recognise and engage; dissect and test; analogise and innovate; incorporate and harmonise; commit and apply; reinforce and insulate; align and refine), not by the names. Cross-check against the survey: the favourites should sit opposite the dislikes. When they do not, that is itself a question.

**The missing axis, live.** As the person talks (about a dream, a conflict, a decision), score their words against the lexicon and track which of the seven axes of value are being seen and which are not. An axis counts as unseen when its tools and weapons are both absent from the account of a situation where the situation plainly calls for it. Then the app asks the two questions:

1. *Who do you know who is good at* [that axis, described by what it does]?
2. *What would they likely do here?*

The person's answer is the reframing, in their own words, from a person they already respect. The app never supplies the reframing itself.

## 3. Question rules

The app asks; it does not tell. The questions must not lead, and they must not be self-fulfilling.

- **Ask for a person, not an opinion.** "Who do you know who is patient?" not "Do you think you are impatient?"
- **Ask for what someone would do, not what the person should do.** "What would she do with this?" not "Should you be more patient?"
- **Ask for the three words, not the trait.** For a person in a dream or an account: "What three words come to mind for them?" never "Is this person controlling?"
- **Ask for the dislike, not the taboo.** "Which of these do you like least in other people?" never "Which approach are you avoiding?" The person names a weapon; the app does the mapping and does not announce it until it can be checked.
- **Ask about the situation before the emotion.** "What happened just before?" not "Were you angry?"
- **Never say what the dream means.** The dream proposes, waking life disposes. The app offers the grammar's reading as a hypothesis and ends with one checkable thing.
- **Never use resonance as evidence.** "That sounds right" is weak evidence. The app asks for a case: "When did that last happen?"
- **One question at a time, and the app waits.** The stress books: an overruled alarm gets louder, and a person interrogated gets defended.
- **Contrary elements first.** Before the app draws a conclusion it names the element that pushes the other way and asks about it.
- **End every session with a criterion.** One thing to watch for, one person to ask, one re-check time.
- **Never point out a fallacy when it happens.** Record it with its context and wait until the person asks the app something; then ask the chain that lets them see it (`docs/context-and-definitions.md`, section 5).

A question provokes insight when the person, answering it, says something they had not said before. The app tracks that: an answer that only restates the account gets a different follow-up than one that adds a person, a time, or a contrary element.

## 4. How the books apply, and how the app sees that

The app carries an index of the situations each book treats, keyed by lexicon hits and by the folder the passage landed in:

| Signal in the account | Source that applies | What the app asks or offers |
|---|---|---|
| Weapon words for one approach cluster | *Beyond Flat Land* ch. 13 and 28 (shadow forms; excess and deficiency) | The two-pole question: what would the competent use of this tool look like here? |
| A person described by one mechanism ("he's just X") | ch. 13 shadow Deconstruction | Three words for the person, not one. |
| A dream with two or more heart or mind markers | *Four Rivers* | The reading method, step by step. |
| A person, relationship or love held with proof, ledger, or verdict | *Postpartum* ch. 26; *Alphas* courtroom | Whose eyes is the person seeing this through? What would doing, rather than proving, look like? |
| An alarm with no criterion; checking that does not end | *Alphas* and *Postpartum* on levels and criteria | Name the number, the criterion, the re-check time. |
| The most meaningful thing has no urgency | *Postpartum* on the top fifth | When is its Thursday at seven? |
| Joy without peace, or peace without joy | *Beyond Flat Land* two questions; *Postpartum* two compasses | Which of the two questions is being answered by "the number went up"? |
| The same grain returning (a recurring dream, a recurring fight) | *Postpartum* ch. 26; *Four Rivers* series of three | What has been held with the wrong tools? |
| An emotion that has to scream | `docs/seven-emotions.md` | The verb the emotion proposes, and the criterion that lets it stop. |

The books never appear as quotations unless asked. They appear as the question chosen.

## 5. The sequence of a session

1. Listen. Transcribe and file into folders.
2. Score. Lexicon hits per tool and weapon; which axes are present.
3. Check. Contrary elements, the folder history (has this person, this dream, this fight been here before).
4. Choose one question by the table above, obeying the rules in section 3.
5. Ask it by voice, and wait.
6. Repeat until the person has said something new, or has named a person and what that person would do.
7. End with a criterion and file it in Alarms or Waking life.

## 6. What the app stores about the person

- Their favourite three approaches (chosen by description).
- Their three most disliked weapons, and the derived taboo and load-bearing pair.
- The people they have named as good at each axis, with what those people would do. This list is the person's own reframing library, in their own words, and it grows.
- The running lexicon profile, so the app can see when an axis that is usually present goes missing in one account, which is the most telling case.
